Warm-upA three-piece function

Find the one-sided limits of this function at 4 and at 9.

\[f(x) = \begin{cases} 3x + 1, & \text{if } x \le 4 \\ 15 - x, & \text{if } 4 < x < 9 \\ x - \sqrt{x}, & \text{if } x \ge 9\end{cases}\]
Show solution

First sort the number line into its three regions: \(3x+1\) to the left of 4, \(15 - x\) between 4 and 9, and \(x - \sqrt{x}\) from 9 on. Then each one-sided limit uses the piece on its own side.

  1. At 4, left\[\lim_{x\to 4^{-}}(3x+1) = 12 + 1 = 13\]
  2. At 4, right\[\lim_{x\to 4^{+}}(15 - x) = 15 - 4 = 11\]
  3. At 413 and 11 are different, so \(\lim_{x\to 4}f(x)\) does not exist
  4. At 9, left\[\lim_{x\to 9^{-}}(15 - x) = 15 - 9 = 6\]
  5. At 9, right\[\lim_{x\to 9^{+}}\left(x - \sqrt{x}\right) = 9 - \sqrt{9} = 9 - 3 = 6\]
  6. At 9both sides give 6, so \(\lim_{x\to 9}f(x) = 6\)

The middle piece is used twice

\(15 - x\) supplies the right limit at 4 and the left limit at 9. A piece in the middle of a case-defined function always does double duty like this, which is why the number-line sketch is worth drawing before you compute anything.

DefinitionInfinite limits

Sometimes you will see a statement of the form

\[\lim_{x\to c} f(x) = \infty\]

This says: the limit of \(f(x)\) as \(x \to c\) does not exist, but there is a clear pattern in its behavior: \(f(x)\) grows large and positive. If instead \(f(x)\) grows large and negative, we write \(\lim_{x\to c}f(x) = -\infty\).

“= ∞” is a description, not a number

Writing \(\lim f(x) = \infty\) does not mean the limit exists and happens to equal a very large number. It means the limit does not exist, and tells you how it fails to exist.

On the graph of \(f\), infinite limits show up as vertical asymptotes.

Worth memorizing

  • The line \(x = c\) is a vertical asymptote for \(f\) if at least one of the one-sided limits at \(c\) is infinite.

For example, \(f(x) = \ln x\) has a vertical asymptote at \(x = 0\), since \(\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}\ln x = -\infty\). There is no left-hand limit at all, because \(\ln x\) is not defined for \(x < 0\), but one side is enough.

When working with infinite limits you must pay careful attention to the sign of the function. There are three possibilities.

Three outcomes

  • large and positive on both sides: \(\infty\)
  • large and negative on both sides: \(-\infty\)
  • large and of variable sign: neither applies, so the most we can say is that the limit does not exist (DNE)

MethodWorking out the sign

General approach. You may get an infinite limit when the denominator approaches zero but the numerator does not. When that happens, study the sign of each part to decide between \(\infty\) and \(-\infty\).

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{x - 3}{5x^2}\).

Show solution
  1. Numerator\(x - 3 \to 0 - 3 = -3\), so it is negative
  2. Denominator\(5x^2 \to 5\cdot 0^2 = 0\), but that alone does not say whether it is positive or negative
  3. Its sign\(x^2 > 0\) for every \(x \ne 0\), so the denominator is positive
  4. Combine(negative) / (positive and close to 0)

Answer−∞

Because \(x^2\) is positive on both sides of 0, the sign does not depend on the direction of approach, and the two-sided statement is fine.

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0^{-}}\frac{4 - x}{x^3}\) and \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}\frac{4 - x}{x^3}\).

Show solution

Note that \(x\) approaches 0 from a specified side. That may be important, and here it is.

  1. Numerator\(4 - x \to 4 - 0 = 4\), so it is positive and not close to zero
  2. Denominator\(x^3 \to 0^3 = 0\), which does not tell us its sign
  3. From the left\(x\) is negative, since it is to the left of 0, and that makes \(x^3\) negative as well
  4. Left result(positive) / (negative and close to 0) gives \(-\infty\)
  5. From the right\(x > 0\), so \(x^3 > 0\): (positive) / (positive and close to 0) gives \(\infty\)

Answer−∞ from the left, ∞ from the right

Compare with the first example. There the denominator was \(x^2\), positive on both sides; here it is \(x^3\), which changes sign at 0. An even power hides the direction of approach, an odd power does not.

Poll question from class

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 2}\frac{x}{x^2 - 4}\).

Show solution
  1. Numerator\(x \to 2\): positive, not close to 0
  2. Denominator\(x^2 - 4 \to 4 - 4 = 0\), which does not tell us its sign
  3. Its signis \(x^2 - 4\) positive or negative when \(x\) is close to 2? It could be either way: it depends on whether \(x < 2\) or \(x > 2\)
  4. Conclusion(positive) / (close to 0, undetermined sign)

AnswerDNE

To see the two sides explicitly, factor: \(x^2 - 4 = (x-2)(x+2)\). Near 2 the factor \(x+2\) is about 4, so the sign is the sign of \(x - 2\). For \(x\) slightly below 2 the denominator is negative and the quotient heads to \(-\infty\); slightly above 2 it is positive and the quotient heads to \(\infty\). Opposite signs, so the two-sided limit does not exist.

The denominator's sign is the whole problem

In all three examples the numerator's sign was obvious, and the denominator went to 0. So the work is always the same: decide the sign of the denominator just to the left and just to the right of \(c\). Factoring it usually makes that immediate.

DefinitionLimits at infinity

Think about what “long-term behavior” means: what happens as the time variable becomes very large. The notation for this is

\[\lim_{x\to\infty} f(x) \qquad\text{and, similarly,}\qquad \lim_{x\to -\infty} f(x)\]

the limit at (positive) infinity and the limit at negative infinity. Read the subscript first: it tells you which kind of limit you are looking at. When these limits exist, they appear on the graph as horizontal asymptotes.

Two ideas, two different asymptotes

An infinite limit, \(\lim_{x\to c}f(x) = \pm\infty\), is about the output blowing up, and gives a vertical asymptote. A limit at infinity, \(\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}f(x)\), is about the input running away, and gives a horizontal asymptote. The infinity is on the opposite side of the arrow in each case.

Worth memorizing

  • When \(x\) is large, the greatest power of \(x\) dominates all the other powers, and so determines the behavior of \(f(x)\).

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2x - x^3}{100 + 3x^3 - x^2}\).

Show solution
  1. Topthe highest power is \(-x^3\)
  2. Bottomthe highest power is \(3x^3\)
  3. Keep thosebecause \(x \to \infty\), ignore the smaller powers: \[\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{-x^3}{3x^3}\]
  4. Simplify\(x^3\) cancels: \[= -\frac{1}{3}\]

Answer−1/3

The 100 in the denominator looks large but is irrelevant: once \(x\) is in the thousands, \(3x^3\) is in the billions.

What happens when the highest powers on top and bottom are not equal?

Find a) \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to -\infty}\frac{2x - x^4}{100 + 3x^3 - x^2}\)   and   b) \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2x - x^4}{100 + 3x^3 - x^5}\).

Show solution
  1. a) Keep\[\lim_{x\to -\infty}\frac{-x^4}{3x^3}\]
  2. a) Simplify\[= \lim_{x\to -\infty}\frac{-x}{3}\]
  3. a) Decide\(x\) is large and negative, so \(-x\) is large and positive: the limit is \(\infty\)
  4. b) Keep\[\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{-x^4}{-x^5}\]
  5. b) Simplify\[= \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1}{x}\]
  6. b) Decidea fixed number over a huge one is tiny: at \(x = 1{,}000{,}000\), \(\frac1x = 0.000001\). The limit is \(0\)

Answer∞;   0

The three cases for a fraction at infinity

  • Same highest power on top and bottom: the limit is the ratio of their coefficients.
  • Bigger power on top: an \(x\) is left in the numerator, so the limit is \(\infty\) or \(-\infty\). Settle which by the sign.
  • Bigger power on the bottom: an \(x\) is left in the denominator, so the limit is \(0\).

Watch the sign when x → −∞

In (a), the leftover \(\frac{-x}{3}\) has a minus sign, and \(x\) is negative, so the two cancel to give \(+\infty\). Treating \(x\) as positive out of habit gives \(-\infty\), the wrong answer. Always ask what sign \(x\) itself has before concluding.

ReferenceThe whole strategy, 9.1 and 9.2

Everything from the last two lectures, as one procedure for finding \(\lim_{x\to c}f(x)\).

  1. Try to plug in \(x = c\), if \(c\) is a number. For limits as \(x \to \pm\infty\), go to step 7.
  2. If \(f(c)\) exists and \(f\) is not case-defined, the limit is \(f(c)\). If \(f\) is case-defined, go to step 6.
  3. If \(f(c)\) does not exist, it is because a denominator turns to 0 when \(x = c\). Does the numerator also turn to 0? If yes, go to step 4; if no, go to step 5.
  4. Factor both numerator and denominator, and cancel \((x - c)\), or whatever factor is responsible for them being 0. Return to step 1.
  5. The limit is \(\infty\), \(-\infty\), or DNE, depending on the sign of the expression: consistently positive gives \(\infty\), consistently negative gives \(-\infty\), inconsistent gives DNE. Use the algebra of signs, for example (negative)/(negative) = (positive). Remember that \(x \to c^{+}\) means \(x > c\), approaching from the right, and \(x \to c^{-}\) means \(x < c\), from the left.
  6. For case-defined functions, compare the two one-sided limits. If they are the same number, that number is the limit; otherwise the limit does not exist.
  7. Limits at infinity are decided by the highest powers of \(x\). If there is a fraction, find the highest power on top and on the bottom, ignore the rest, and simplify. Then use the fact that \(x\) is large and positive, or large and negative, to conclude. \(\lim f(x) = \infty\) means \(f(x)\) gets very large and positive; \(\lim f(x) = -\infty\) means very large and negative.
