Warm-upOne more exponent problem

Simplify \(5\left(2x^{3/5}\right)^4\).

Show solution

The exponent 4 applies to everything inside the parentheses, and only to what is inside them. The 5 out front is not raised to anything.

  1. Distribute\((ab)^4 = a^4b^4\), so \[5 \cdot 2^4 \cdot \left(x^{3/5}\right)^4\]
  2. Coefficient\(2^4 = 16\), and \(5 \cdot 16 = 80\)
  3. Exponentsa power of a power multiplies: \[\frac{3}{5}\cdot 4 = \frac{3}{5}\cdot\frac{4}{1} = \frac{12}{5}\]
  4. Combine\[80x^{12/5}\]

Answer80x¹²∕⁵

The exponent distributes over multiplication, not addition

\((ab)^4 = a^4b^4\) is true, because \((ab)^4 = ab\cdot ab\cdot ab\cdot ab\) and you can reorder the factors. But \((a+b)^4 \ne a^4 + b^4\). Grouping what is being raised to the fourth power is the first thing to get right.

OrientationStatic and dynamic

Today we begin calculus. Everything up to now was pre-calculus or algebra. Calculus is what you need to work with quantities that have variable rates of change.

The shift in how to read \(x\)

  • Algebra is static: in an equation with \(x\), we think of \(x\) as some fixed number.
  • Calculus is dynamic: we think of \(x\) as changing, and ask how an expression containing \(x\) is affected by that change.

The definition of a limit is built on that dynamic reading. We say a function \(f(x)\) has limit \(L\) as \(x \to c\) if its values \(f(x)\) approach \(L\) when \(x\) approaches \(c\), from either side.

Picture \(c\) as a fixed number on the horizontal axis and \(L\) a fixed number on the vertical axis. The points \((x, f(x))\) float around \((c, L)\), getting closer and closer. We write

\[\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L\]

It may happen that \(f(x)\) does not approach any particular number as \(x \to c\). In that case the limit does not exist.

DefinitionApproaching versus equalling

Before computing anything, be clear on the difference between \(x \to c\) and \(x = c\).

Two different questions

  • \(x = 2\) gives the value \(f(2)\): plug 2 into the formula. This is algebra.
  • \(x \to 2\) means \(x\) is not equal to 2 but is close to it. The limit \(\lim\limits_{x\to 2}f(x)\) may be the same number as \(f(2)\), or a different one. This is calculus.

The two can come apart in both directions. Sometimes \(f(c)\) exists but \(\lim_{x\to c}f(x)\) does not. Sometimes \(f(c)\) is not even defined, yet the limit exists.

Let \(f(x)\) be \(x\) rounded down to an integer, so \(f(7.55) = 7\), \(f(5.03) = 5\), \(f(4) = 4\). Then \(f(2) = 2\), but it is not true that \(f(x)\) approaches 2 as \(x \to 2\). Why?

Show solution

Test values on each side of 2.

  1. Just above\(f(2.01) = 2\)
  2. Just below\(f(1.99) = 1\)
  3. Conclusionvalues near 2 are not all close to one number, so \(\lim\limits_{x\to 2}f(x)\) does not exist

AnswerThe limit does not exist, even though f(2) = 2.

Approaching from the right gives values of 2, but approaching from the left gives values of 1. Since the definition requires the values to approach a single number from either side, there is no limit.

This example is exactly what motivates the next idea.

DefinitionOne-sided limits

For some functions the behavior of \(f(x)\) depends on whether \(x < c\) or \(x > c\). When it does, we describe each side separately.

Worth memorizing

  • \(x \to c^{+}\) means \(x\) approaches \(c\) from the right, so \(x > c\)
  • \(x \to c^{-}\) means \(x\) approaches \(c\) from the left, so \(x < c\)

So \(\lim\limits_{x\to 4^{+}}f(x)\) describes \(f(x)\) when \(x\) is a little more than 4, and \(\lim\limits_{x\to 4^{-}}f(x)\) describes it when \(x\) is a little less than 4.

For the rounding-down function above,

\[\lim_{x \to 2^{+}} f(x) = 2 \qquad\text{but}\qquad \lim_{x \to 2^{-}} f(x) = 1\]

The minus sign is not a negative number

“\(x \to 2^{-}\)” has nothing to do with the number \(-2\). The superscript marks a direction of approach, not a sign. It just means coming at 2 from the left.

How one-sided and two-sided limits relate

  • If the one-sided limits are different, the two-sided limit does not exist. There is a jump.
  • If the one-sided limits are equal, the two-sided limit agrees with them. No jump.
  • If the two-sided limit exists, both one-sided limits agree with it.

Worked exampleLimits with piecewise functions

Piecewise functions are where one-sided limits earn their keep: the formula genuinely changes at the boundary, so you must use the piece that matches the side you are approaching from.

For \[f(x) = \begin{cases} x^2 + 1, & \text{if } x \le 3 \\ 4x - 2, & \text{if } x > 3\end{cases}\] find \(\lim\limits_{x\to 3^{-}}f(x)\), \(\lim\limits_{x\to 3^{+}}f(x)\), and \(\lim\limits_{x\to 3}f(x)\).

Show solution
  1. From the left\(x \to 3^{-}\) means \(x < 3\), which is the first case: \[\lim\limits_{x\to 3^{-}}\left(x^2+1\right) = 9 + 1 = 10\]
  2. From the right\(x \to 3^{+}\) means \(x > 3\), the second case: \[\lim\limits_{x\to 3^{+}}(4x-2) = 12 - 2 = 10\]
  3. Two-sidedboth sides give 10, so \[\lim_{x\to 3}f(x) = 10\]

Answer10, 10, and 10

Poll question from class

Now change the function a little. For \[g(x) = \begin{cases} x^2 - 1, & \text{if } x \le 3 \\ 4x + 2, & \text{if } x > 3\end{cases}\] find the same three limits.

Show solution
  1. From the left\[\lim_{x\to 3^{-}}\left(x^2-1\right) = 9 - 1 = 8\]
  2. From the right\[\lim_{x\to 3^{+}}(4x+2) = 12 + 2 = 14\]
  3. Two-sidedthe two sides disagree, so \(\lim\limits_{x\to 3}g(x)\) does not exist

Answer8, 14, and DNE

Saying \(x\) approaches 3 does not say from which side, so we cannot tell whether \(g(x)\) is close to 8 or to 14. With no single answer, there is no limit.

Two small changes, a completely different answer

\(f\) and \(g\) differ only in two constants, and that is enough to turn a limit of 10 into no limit at all. Always evaluate both sides before answering; the two-sided limit is a conclusion you draw afterwards, not something you can read off one formula.

Note also that \(g(3) = 8\) is perfectly well defined. A function can have a value at a point and still have no limit there.

ToolRules for finding limits

Most limits are far less dramatic than the ones above. For well-behaved functions, taking a limit is the same as substituting.

For positive powers of \(x\), the limit as \(x \to a\) is just the value at \(a\). For example \(\lim\limits_ {x\to 3}x^2 = 3^2 = 9\); the graph has no jumps.

Worth memorizing

  • Limits distribute over sums, differences and products.
  • Constants can be pulled out of a limit.

Find \(\lim\limits_ {x\to -3}\left(4x^2 - 2x - 5\right)\).

Show solution
  1. Split\[\lim_{x\to-3}\left(4x^2\right) - \lim_{x\to-3}(2x) - \lim_{x\to-3}5\]
  2. Substitute\[4(-3)^2 - 2(-3) - 5\]
  3. Compute\[36 + 6 - 5 = 37\]

Answer37

Watch the signs: \((-3)^2 = 9\) so the first term is \(+36\), and \(-2(-3) = +6\).

This shows that for any polynomial, the limit is just the function value. The same works for square roots:

\[\lim_{x\to 3}\sqrt{2x+10} = \sqrt{16} = 4\]

Why this is not anticlimactic

The fact that substitution works for many functions is exactly what makes the exceptions worth naming. The interesting limits are the ones where substitution fails, and the rest of this lecture is about those.

MethodQuotients and the 0/0 form

With quotients you have to be careful. The limit can be distributed across a fraction only if doing so does not create division by 0.

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 4}\frac{x^2 - x - 2}{3x - 6}\).

Show solution
  1. Numerator\[4^2 - 4 - 2 = 16 - 6 = 10\]
  2. Denominator\[3(4) - 6 = 6\]
  3. Dividethe denominator is not 0, so the split is legal: \[\frac{10}{6} = \frac{5}{3}\]

Answer5/3

Now find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 2}\frac{x^2 - x - 2}{3x - 6}\).

Show solution

Try the same approach first and watch it fail.

  1. Numerator\[2^2 - 2 - 2 = 0\]
  2. Denominator\[3(2) - 6 = 0\]
  3. Stuckboth approach 0, so splitting the limit gives \(\frac{0}{0}\), which is not a meaningful computation
  4. Factor\[\frac{x^2-x-2}{3x-6} = \frac{(x-2)(x+1)}{3(x-2)}\]
  5. Cancel\(x \to 2\) means \(x \ne 2\), so \(x - 2 \ne 0\) and it may be cancelled: \[= \frac{x+1}{3}\]
  6. Substitute\[\frac{2+1}{3} = \frac{3}{3} = 1\]

Answer1

The 0/0 indeterminate form

When both numerator and denominator approach 0, the expression is called a 0/0 indeterminate form. The name emphasizes that at a glance you cannot tell what the limit is, or whether it exists at all.

It really can be anything. Dividing \(0.001\) by \(0.00005\) gives 20, and both of those are close to 0.

Failing to find a limit is not proof that there isn't one

When distributing the limit fails, do not conclude that the limit does not exist. We failed to find something; that is not the same as showing there is nothing to find. Instead, factor and cancel before taking the limit.

Two things to remember when cancelling

  • A common factor can be cancelled, not just any common part of two expressions.
  • Look for \((x - c)\) as a factor, where \(c\) is the number \(x\) is approaching.

That second point is a reliable hint. If substituting \(c\) makes both top and bottom zero, then \((x-c)\) divides both of them, so it is there to be found.
