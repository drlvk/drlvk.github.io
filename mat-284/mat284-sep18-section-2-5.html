Why this section matters later

Exponentials and logarithms are the last piece of pre-calculus before limits and derivatives. Many calculus problems require mastery of the formulas in this lecture, so gaps here get expensive.

OrientationWhy exponentials

To be ready for calculus we need to understand exponential and logarithmic functions properly. Exponentials have many applications:

  • Compound interest
  • Population growth models, a better fit for bacteria than for humans
  • Epidemics
  • Radioactive decay

But for the purposes of this course, two things matter most: the algebraic properties of exponential functions, and the shape of their graphs. Everything below is one or the other.

DefinitionDefinition and base

An exponential function has the form

\[f(x) = b^x\]

where \(b\) is a fixed number called the base. The variable is in the exponent, and that is where the name comes from.

Exponential or not

  • \(2^x\) and \(10^x\) are exponential, with bases 2 and 10. So is \((0.3)^x\).
  • \(x^2\) and \(x^{10}\) are not. The variable is in the base, not the exponent. These are power functions.

The base of an exponential is always positive, and never equal to 1. There is a big difference between the two cases that remain:

Growth against decay

  • \(b > 1\) gives exponential growth
  • \(0 < b < 1\) gives exponential decay
The same powers for base 10 and base 0.1

Worth memorizing

  • \(f(0) = b^0 = 1\), no matter what the base is
  • \(f(1) = b^1 = b\)
  • \(f(-1) = b^{-1} = \dfrac{1}{b}\)

That last one is worth a second look. Why is \(b^{-1} = \frac{1}{b}\)? Because \(b^{-1}\cdot b = b^{-1+1} = b^0 = 1\), and the number you multiply \(b\) by to get 1 is \(\frac{1}{b}\). The same reasoning gives \(2^{-3} = \frac{1}{2^3} = \frac{1}{8}\).

Always above the axis

In every case the graph lies above the horizontal axis: all values of an exponential function are positive. It flattens toward the axis on one side, getting arbitrarily close without touching, and shoots up on the other. It never crosses.

MethodReading the base off a graph

Given the graph of \(f(x) = b^x\), how do you recover \(b\)? Use one of the key values above. The point at \(x = 1\) gives \(b\) directly, and the point at \(x = -1\) gives \(\frac{1}{b}\).

From WeBWorK. (a) A graph of \(f(x) = b^x\) passes through \((1, 6)\). What is \(b\)?

(b) Another graph of \(f(x) = b^x\) passes through \((-1, 4)\). What is \(b\)?

Show solution
  1. a) Read\(x = 1\) gives \(f(1) = 6\)
  2. a) But\(f(1) = b^1 = b\), so \(b = 6\)
  3. b) Read\(x = -1\) gives \(f(-1) = 4\)
  4. b) But\(f(-1) = b^{-1} = \dfrac{1}{b}\), so \(\dfrac{1}{b} = 4\)
  5. b) Solve\[b = \frac{1}{4}\]

Answerb = 6, and b = 1/4

The answer to (b) is not 4

A falling graph must have \(0 < b < 1\), so \(b = 4\) is impossible before you do any algebra. Check the shape against your answer: rising means \(b > 1\), falling means \(b\) between 0 and 1. And the base is never negative.

ToolThe exponent laws

To work with exponentials we need the rules for combining exponents. Each one answers the same question: how many copies of \(b\) are being multiplied here?

Worth memorizing

  • \(b^x b^y = b^{x+y}\)
  • \(\dfrac{b^x}{b^y} = b^{x-y}\)
  • \(\left(b^x\right)^y = b^{xy}\)

Why the first one is true, in one line: \(3^5 \cdot 3^7\) is five copies of 3 times seven copies of 3, so twelve copies in all, which is \(3^{12}\). The third works the same way: \(\left(3^2\right)^3 = (3\cdot 3)(3\cdot 3)(3\cdot 3) = 3^6\).

Write each as a single power.

a) \(3^x \cdot 3^{2x}\)    b) \(9^x \cdot 3^{5x}\)    c) \(3 \cdot 3^{4x}\)    d) \(3^{4x} \cdot \left(\sqrt{3}\right)^x\)

Show solution
  1. a)\[3^x \cdot 3^{2x} = 3^{x+2x} = 3^{3x} = \left(3^3\right)^x = 27^x\]
  2. b) Firstget a common base: \(9^x = \left(3^2\right)^x = 3^{2x}\)
  3. b) Then\[3^{2x} \cdot 3^{5x} = 3^{7x}\]
  4. c)\(3 = 3^1\), so \[3^1 \cdot 3^{4x} = 3^{1 + 4x}\]
  5. d) First\(\sqrt{3} = 3^{0.5}\), so \(\left(\sqrt{3}\right)^x = 3^{0.5x}\)

These laws matter just as much for powers of \(x\), which are not exponential functions but obey the same rules.

In (b) and (c) the outside exponent hits the coefficient too: \(4^2 = 16\) and \(2^3 = 8\). Leaving the 4 or the 2 untouched is the usual slip.

MethodWhat does and does not distribute

Exponentiation distributes over multiplication and division:

\[(ab)^x = a^x b^x, \qquad \left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^x = \frac{a^x}{b^x}\]

It does not distribute over + or −

\[(a + b)^x \ne a^x + b^x\]

There is no rule that breaks a sum inside an exponent into separate pieces. This is the single most common error in the section.

Changing the sign of the exponent turns an expression into its reciprocal, \(b^{-x} = \dfrac{1}{b^x}\). In more detail, using the division law:

\[\frac{1}{b^x} = \frac{b^0}{b^x} = b^{0-x} = b^{-x}\]

So the graph of the exponential with base \(\frac{1}{b}\) is the mirror image, across the vertical axis, of the graph with base \(b\). That is exactly the relationship between \(10^x\) and \((0.1)^x\) in the figure above: \((0.1)^x = \left(\frac{1}{10}\right)^x = 10^{-x}\), and replacing \(x\) by \(-x\) flips a graph left to right.

In general the function \(f(x) = P\,b^x\) can be recognized from its graph using two values:

Worth memorizing

  • \(f(0) = P\,b^0 = P\)
  • \(f(1) = P\,b\)

So \(P\) is the height at which the graph crosses the vertical axis. It describes exponential growth or decay with initial amount \(P\).

Reading P and b in that order

Read \(P\) off the vertical intercept first, then use \(f(1) = Pb\) to get \(b\) by dividing. Trying to read \(b\) straight off \(f(1)\) only works when \(P = 1\).

Worked exampleCompound interest

A person deposits \(P\) dollars, the principal, into an account earning 2% annually. Each year the balance is multiplied by \(1.02\), so after \(t\) years it is

\[P \cdot (1.02)^t\]

If the interest is compounded more often than once a year, each period pays a fraction of the annual rate, and there are more periods.

Worth memorizing

  • \[A(t) = P\left(1 + \frac{r}{m}\right)^{mt} \qquad\text{(compound interest)}\]
  • \(r\) is the annual rate, as a decimal: \(0.02\) for 2%
  • \(m\) is the number of compounding periods per year: 12 for monthly

For instance, with monthly compounding after 5 years the exponent is \(mt = 12 \cdot 5 = 60\), giving \(P\left(1 + \frac{0.02}{12}\right)^{60}\).

Now push \(m\) upward: compound every week, every day, every hour, every second. The balance does not run away; it settles on a limit, and that limit is written with the number \(e = 2.718\ldots\)

Worth memorizing

  • \[A(t) = P e^{rt} \qquad\text{(continuous compound interest)}\]

Your TI 83/84 has two exponentials on the keypad, \(10^x\) and \(e^x\). Both are second functions, printed in blue above their keys, so press 2nd first. One has base 10; the other has base \(e\).

Start with $1,000 at a 2% annual rate. How much is there after 10 years, with

(a) annual compounding,   (b) monthly compounding,   (c) continuous compounding?

Show solution
  1. a) Annual\[1000(1.02)^{10} = 1218.99\]
  2. b) Monthly\(m = 12\) and \(mt = 120\): \[1000\left(1 + \frac{0.02}{12}\right)^{120} = 1221.20\]
  3. c) Continuous\(rt = 0.02 \cdot 10 = 0.2\): \[1000e^{0.2} = 1221.40\]

Answer$1,218.99  ·  $1,221.20  ·  $1,221.40

What the three numbers tell you

They increase, and they increase by less and less: $1,218.99, then $1,221.20, then $1,221.40. More frequent compounding always pays more, but the gain shrinks fast, and continuous compounding is the ceiling it is heading for. Going from annual to monthly buys $2.21; going from monthly all the way to continuous buys 20 cents more.

So the continuous formula is not an exotic special case. It is the simple answer to “what if we compounded constantly,” and it is close to any frequent compounding.

Two things to get right

Use the rate as a decimal: \(r = 0.02\), never \(2\). And the exponent in the compound formula is \(mt\), not \(t\): with monthly compounding over 10 years it is \(120\), not \(10\).

Worked exampleThree population models

From WeBWorK. A town has a population of 1200 people at time \(t = 0\). In each case write a formula for the population as a function of the year.

(a) The population increases by 80 people per year.

(b) The population grows continuously with a relative growth rate of 4%.

(c) The population increases by 4% every year.

Show solution

Each phrase points at one of the three models. The initial amount is 1200 in all three.

  1. a)a fixed number of people per year is linear: \[P(t) = 1200 + 80t\]
  2. b)“continuously” means \(Pe^{rt}\) with \(r = 0.04\): \[P(t) = 1200e^{0.04t}\]
  3. c)a fixed percentage each year is annual compounding: \[P(t) = 1200(1.04)^t\]

Answer1200 + 80t  ·  1200e⁰·⁰⁴ᵗ  ·  1200(1.04)ᵗ

Adding versus multiplying

“80 people per year” is something you add, so it gives \(+80t\). “4% every year” is something you multiply by, so it gives \((1.04)^t\). Deciding which of those two the wording describes is the whole problem.

And (b) against (c): both are 4% growth, but continuous compounding runs slightly ahead of annual, exactly as in the interest example. After 30 years the town has about 3,984 people under (b) and about 3,892 under (c).

Linear wins early, exponential wins eventually

Notice the crossing in the figure. At 10 years the linear model gives the largest population by far, 2,000 against about 1,790 and 1,776. The exponentials only overtake it around year 26. Exponential growth being “fast” is a statement about the long run, not about every year.
