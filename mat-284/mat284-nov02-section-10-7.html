Warm-upTwo chain rule problems

Chain rules in play

  • \(\left((\square)^r\right)' = r(\square)^{r-1} \cdot (\square)'\)
  • \(\left(e^{\square}\right)' = e^{\square} \cdot (\square)'\)
  • \((\ln \square)' = \dfrac{1}{\square} \cdot (\square)'\), or \(\dfrac{(\square)'}{\square}\)

A tip for the first one: when the denominator is raised to some power, we can turn the quotient into a product, \(\dfrac{T}{B^p} = T \cdot B^{-p}\). This costs nothing, and the product rule is simpler.

IdeaPrice, demand, and revenue

The new topic for today: using calculus to study the relation between price, demand, and revenue. We know that revenue is (price) · (quantity), but this does not mean that raising the price will increase the revenue, because the demand (quantity sold) may drop.

Consider two scenarios. (A) The price went up by 5% and the demand dropped by 2%. (B) The price went up by 5% and the demand dropped by 9%. What will happen to the revenue in each scenario?

Show solution

Up by 5% means new = old + 5% of old = (1.05)(old). Dropped by 2% means new = (0.98)(old).

  1. (A)\[\frac{\text{new } R}{\text{old } R} = \frac{(\text{new } p)(\text{new } x)}{(\text{old } p)(\text{old } x)} = (1.05)(0.98) \approx 1.029\] so revenue is up by 2.9%
  2. (B)\[\frac{\text{new } R}{\text{old } R} = (1.05)(0.91) = 0.9555\] so revenue is down by about 4.45%, since \((0.9555 - 1) \cdot 100 = -4.45\)

Answer(A) revenue up 2.9%  ·  (B) revenue down about 4.45%

The terms “elastic demand” (B) and “inelastic demand” (A) should make intuitive sense in this context. Some products are easier to go without than others.

Worth memorizing

  • Elastic demand ⇔ sensitive to changes of price ⇔ increasing the price will decrease revenue
  • Inelastic demand ⇔ not as sensitive ⇔ increasing the price will increase revenue

The borderline case is called unit elasticity: as the price goes up by 1%, the demand also drops by 1%.

ToolRelative rate of change

When discussing changes in prices or quantities, we often use percentages, which make such changes easier to grasp. Compare two statements:

  • Annual sales are up by 2,571,500 dollars.
  • Annual sales are up by 3.7%.

However, percentage changes are not as convenient in complex computations, because they add a lot of steps like “multiply by 100 to convert to %.” In this section, we will consider relative changes instead: dividing the amount of change by the original amount, but without multiplying by 100%. So in the example above one could say “the relative change is 0.037.” A relative change is a percent change without the factor of 100.

Definition

The relative rate of change of a function \(f(x)\) is

  • \[\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}\]

Find the relative rate of change of (a) \(f(x) = x^2 + 10x + 70\) and (b) \(f(x) = x\).

Show solution
  1. (a)\[\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)} = \frac{(x^2 + 10x + 70)'}{x^2 + 10x + 70} = \frac{2x + 10}{x^2 + 10x + 70}\]
  2. (b)\(f'(x) = 1\), so the relative rate of change is \(\dfrac{1}{x}\)

Answer(a) (2x + 10)/(x² + 10x + 70)   (b) 1/x

The relative rate of change is also known as the logarithmic derivative, because by the logarithmic chain rule

\[\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)} = \big(\ln f(x)\big)'\]

This is occasionally useful, when \(f\) is an exponential function or a product. Exponential functions have a very simple relative rate of change.

Find the relative rate of change of \(f(x) = 30e^{-2x}\).

Show solution

Either find \(f'/f\) directly, or find \(\ln f\) and take the derivative of that.

  1. ln f\(\ln f(x) = \ln\left(30e^{-2x}\right) = \ln(30) + \ln\left(e^{-2x}\right) = \ln(30) - 2x\)
  2. Derivative\(\dfrac{f'}{f} = (\ln 30 - 2x)' = -2\), since \(\ln 30\) is a constant
  3. Checkdirectly: \(f'(x) = 30e^{-2x} \cdot (-2) = -60e^{-2x}\), and \(\dfrac{-60e^{-2x}}{30e^{-2x}} = -2\)

Answer−2

DefinitionElasticity of demand

Definition

The elasticity of demand is

  • \[E = -\frac{\text{relative rate of change of demand}}{\text{relative rate of change of price}} = -\frac{dx/x}{dp/p} = -\frac{p}{x}\,\frac{dx}{dp}\]

Here \(\dfrac{dx}{dp}\) is the derivative of \(x\) with respect to \(p\).

The numerator and denominator have opposite signs, so the minus sign is included in the formula to make elasticity positive. Why opposite signs? When the price goes up, the demand goes down: the two relative changes always move in opposite directions.

To compute the elasticity of demand, we need the price-demand equation, which relates the price \(p\) and the demand \(x\). We solve the price-demand equation for \(x\), so it takes the form \(x = f(p)\). This means we use \(p\) as the input (variable) and \(x\) as the output. Then:

  • the relative rate of change of demand is \(f'(p)/f(p)\);
  • the relative rate of change of price is \(1/p\), as we found for \(f(x) = x\).

Worth memorizing

And thus we get the main formula of this section:

  • \[E = -\frac{p\,f'(p)}{f(p)}\]

In other places you may see it written as \(E = -\dfrac{p}{x}\,\dfrac{dx}{dp}\), which is the same thing in different notation. There are other versions as well.

Based on the function \(f\) and the price level, we get one of three outcomes:

Reading the elasticity
ValueDemand isMeaning
\(E < 1\)inelasticdemand changes less than the price; a price increase will increase revenue
\(E > 1\)elasticdemand changes more than the price; a price increase will decrease revenue
\(E = 1\)unit elasticitythe same relative change

PracticeComputing elasticity

The method: write the price-demand equation as \(x = f(p)\), then use \(E = -\dfrac{p f'}{f}\).

1. Find the elasticity of demand at price \(p = 30\), given the price-demand equation \(2x + p = 40\). Interpret the answer as elastic, inelastic, or unit elasticity.

Show solution
  1. Solve for x\(2x = 40 - p\), so \(x = 20 - \frac12 p = f(p)\), and \(f'(p) = -\frac12\)
  2. Formula\(-p\) times the derivative, over the formula for \(x\): \[E = \frac{-p\left(-\frac12\right)}{20 - \frac12 p} = \frac{p/2}{20 - p/2} = \frac{p}{40 - p}\]
  3. At p = 30\(E = \dfrac{30}{40 - 30} = 3 > 1\)

AnswerE = 3: demand is elastic

What E = 3 says

At \(p = 30\), a 1% increase in price cuts demand by about 3%, so raising the price would lower the revenue. Elasticity depends on the price: for this demand equation it is below 1 at low prices and above 1 at high ones.

2. The price-demand equation is \(x = p^{-3}e^{-2p}\). Find the formula for the elasticity \(E\).

Show solution
  1. f(p)\(f(p) = p^{-3}e^{-2p}\), a product
  2. f′(p)\[f'(p) = (p^{-3})'(e^{-2p}) + p^{-3}\left(e^{-2p}\right)' = -3p^{-4}e^{-2p} + p^{-3}e^{-2p}(-2)\]
  3. Formula\(-p\) times the derivative, over the formula for \(x\): \[E = \frac{-p\left(-3p^{-4}e^{-2p} - 2p^{-3}e^{-2p}\right)}{p^{-3}e^{-2p}} = \frac{p\left(3p^{-4} + 2p^{-3}\right)e^{-2p}}{p^{-3}e^{-2p}}\]
  4. Simplify\(e^{-2p}\) cancels, and dividing by \(p^{-3}\) multiplies by \(p^3\): \[E = p^4\left(3p^{-4} + 2p^{-3}\right) = 3 + 2p\]

AnswerE = 3 + 2p

A shortcut with the logarithmic derivative

Since \(f\) is a product with an exponential, \(\dfrac{f'}{f} = (\ln f)'\) is quicker: \(\ln f(p) = -3\ln p - 2p\), so \(\dfrac{f'}{f} = -\dfrac{3}{p} - 2\), and \(E = -p\left(-\dfrac{3}{p} - 2\right) = 3 + 2p\). Here \(E > 1\) at every positive price, so this demand is always elastic.
