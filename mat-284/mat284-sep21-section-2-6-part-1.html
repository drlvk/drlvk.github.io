Warm-upA continuous growth model

The rat population of a city is given by \(n(t) = 25e^{0.04t}\), where \(t\) is measured in years since 1994 and \(n\) in millions.

(a) What kind of growth model is this?   (b) What was the population in 1994?   (c) What was it in 2006?

Show solution

Match the formula against \(A(t) = Pe^{rt}\) from Friday. Here \(P = 25\) and \(r = 0.04\).

  1. a)the \(e\) marks it as continuous growth, with a 4% relative growth rate
  2. b) Year1994 is \(t = 0\)
  3. b) Value\[n(0) = 25e^0 = 25 \text{ million} = 25{,}000{,}000\]
  4. c) Year\(2006 - 1994 = 12\), so \(t = 12\)
  5. c) Substitute\[n(12) = 25e^{0.04 \cdot 12} = 25e^{0.48}\]
  6. c) Compute\[= 40.40186 \text{ million}\]

Answercontinuous 4% growth; 25,000,000 rats; about 40,401,860 rats

Two things this problem is checking

That you convert the year into \(t\), the years since 1994, rather than substituting 2006. And that you read the units: \(n\) is in millions, so 40.40186 has to be turned back into animals.

Recognizing the model is the (a) part: \(Pe^{rt}\) is continuous, \(P(1+r)^t\) is annual compounding, and \(P + mt\) is linear.

DefinitionThe inverse of an exponential

Last time we studied exponential functions \(f(x) = b^x\), where the base \(b\) is either \(b > 1\) (growth) or \(0 < b < 1\) (decay). The input to an exponential is the exponent, and the output is what you get by raising \(b\) to that power.

Logarithmic functions do exactly the opposite: the input is some power of \(b\), and the output is the exponent in that power. This is an example of an inverse function.

Worth memorizing

  • \(y = \log_b x\)   exactly when   \(b^y = x\)

So taking the logarithm of a number \(x\) answers one question: to what power should we raise \(b\) to get \(x\)? Whenever a logarithm confuses you, ask it in those words.

MethodLogarithms without a calculator

It is worth practising simple logarithms by hand. The exercise makes them less mysterious, and it is what lets you handle logarithms of symbolic expressions later.

Evaluate each without a calculator.

\(\log_{10} 1000\),   \(\log_{10} 10000000\),   \(\log_2 32\),   \(\log_{10} 0.1\),   \(\log_3 9\),   \(\log_7 7\),   \(\log_7 1\)

Show solution

In each case, ask what exponent turns the base into the input.

  1. 1000\(10^3 = 1000\), so \(\log_{10}1000 = 3\)
  2. 10 million\(10^7 = 10{,}000{,}000\), so the answer is \(7\)
  3. 32\(2^5 = 32\), so \(\log_2 32 = 5\)
  4. 0.1\(10^{-1} = 0.1 = \frac{1}{10}\), so \(\log_{10}0.1 = -1\)
  5. 9\(3^2 = 9\), so \(\log_3 9 = 2\)
  6. 7\(7^1 = 7\), so \(\log_7 7 = 1\)
  7. 1\(7^0 = 1\), so \(\log_7 1 = 0\)

Answer3, 7, 5, −1, 2, 1, 0

The last two are worth stating in general, because they hold for every base.

Worth memorizing

  • Since \(b^0 = 1\), we get \(\log_b 1 = 0\) no matter what \(b\) is
  • Since \(b^1 = b\), we get \(\log_b b = 1\)

Beyond cases like these, logarithms generally require a calculator.

A few harder ones, still without a calculator.

a) \(\log_4 2\)    b) \(\log 0.0001\)    c) \(\log_5 0.04\)

Show solution
  1. a)what power of 4 gives 2? \(4^{1/2} = \sqrt{4} = 2\), so \[\log_4 2 = \frac{1}{2}\]
  2. b)\(0.0001 = \frac{1}{10000} = 10^{-4}\), so \[\log 0.0001 = -4\]
  3. c) Rewrite\(0.04 = \frac{4}{100} = \frac{1}{25}\)
  4. c) Recognize\(\frac{1}{25} = \frac{1}{5^2} = 5^{-2}\), so \[\log_5 0.04 = -2\]

Answer1/2,   −4,   −2

Fractions and negatives are allowed answers

A logarithm can come out fractional, as in (a), or negative, as in (b) and (c). Only the input to a logarithm has to be positive; the output can be anything.

For a decimal input, rewriting it as a fraction and then as a negative power is the move: \(0.04 \to \frac{1}{25} \to 5^{-2}\).

Notationlog and ln

Like most calculators, the TI 83/84 has two logarithm buttons, and they cover the two bases that come up constantly.

The two named logarithms

  • Base 10 is the common logarithm, written \(\log\). So \(\log 17\) means \(\log_{10} 17\).
  • Base \(e = 2.71828\ldots\) is the natural logarithm, written \(\ln\). So \(\ln 55\) means \(\log_e 55\).

The significance of the number \(e\) will become clear later in calculus. For now it is the base your calculator's LN key uses.

A bare log means base 10, in this course

Written without a subscript, \(\log\) means base 10 here. Be aware that some fields, and some software, use \(\log\) for the natural logarithm instead. When it matters, write the base.

MethodThe domain of a logarithm

What is the domain of a logarithmic function, and why?

Worth memorizing

  • The domain of \(\log_b x\) is \((0, \infty)\): the input must be positive.

The reason comes straight from the inverse relationship. A logarithm returns the exponent you would feed to \(b^x\), and the output of \(b^x\) is always positive. So there is no exponent that produces zero or a negative number, and \(\log_b 0\) and \(\log_b(-4)\) simply do not exist.

What is the domain of \(f(x) = \log_{10}(3x + 5)\)?

Show solution

The content of the logarithm has to be positive, so set up an inequality on it.

  1. Require\[3x + 5 > 0\]
  2. Subtract 5\[3x > -5\]
  3. Divide by 3\[x > -\frac{5}{3}\]

Answerx > −5/3, that is (−5/3, ∞)

A third rule for domains

Add this to the two from section 2.1. A square root needs its content \(\ge 0\); a denominator must not be 0; and now, the content of a logarithm must be \(> 0\), strictly, with no equality allowed.

MethodSolving log equations

To solve an equation containing a logarithm, rewrite it in exponential form. The two forms say the same thing:

The same statement, two ways

  • logarithmic form: \(y = \log_b x\)
  • exponential form: \(b^y = x\)

Solve a) \(\log_2 x = 7\)    b) \(2\ln(4 - x) - 3 = 0\)    c) \(\log_3(x + 50) = 4\)

Show solution

Two steps each time: isolate the logarithm, then switch to exponential form.

  1. a) Switch\(\log_2 x = 7\) means \[2^7 = x\]
  2. a) Compute\[x = 128\]
  3. b) Isolate\[2\ln(4-x) = 3 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ln(4-x) = \frac{3}{2}\]
  4. b) Switchthe base of \(\ln\) is \(e\): \[e^{3/2} = 4 - x\]
  5. b) Solve\[x = 4 - e^{3/2} \approx -0.4817\]
  6. c) Switch\[3^4 = x + 50\]
  7. c) Solve\[81 = x + 50 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = 31\]

Answerx = 128;   x = 4 − e³∕² ≈ −0.482;   x = 31

Part (c) was the poll question. Note that in (b) you must divide by the 2 and move the 3 before switching forms: the rule applies to \(\log_b(\text{something}) = \text{something}\), with the logarithm alone on one side.

When the unknown is the base

Solve \(\log_x 4 = 2\).

Show solution
  1. Switch\[x^2 = 4\]
  2. Square root\(x = 2\) or \(x = -2\)
  3. Rejecta logarithm's base is either \(>1\) (usual) or between 0 and 1 (unusual), never negative

Answerx = 2

Solve \(\log_4(x^2 + 15) = 2\).

Show solution
  1. Switch\[4^2 = x^2 + 15\]
  2. Simplify\[16 = x^2 + 15 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x^2 = 1\]
  3. Solve\(x = 1\) or \(x = -1\)
  4. Check bothat either value the input is \(1 + 15 = 16 > 0\), so both are valid

Answerx = 1 or x = −1

Why one problem rejects a negative root and the other keeps both

In \(\log_x 4 = 2\) the unknown is the base, and a base can never be negative, so \(-2\) goes. In \(\log_4(x^2+15) = 2\) the unknown sits in the input, and what must be positive is the input \(x^2 + 15\), not \(x\) itself. Since \(x^2 + 15\) is positive for every \(x\), nothing is rejected.

So do not discard negative solutions by reflex. Check what the restriction actually applies to.

MethodSolving exponential equations

The relation \(\log_b x = y \Longleftrightarrow b^y = x\) reads in both directions. We used it to solve for \(x\) when the logarithm was given; now use it to solve for the exponent.

If \(10^y = 42\), what is \(y\)?

Show solution
  1. Switch\[y = \log_{10} 42\]
  2. Calculator\[y \approx 1.62325\]

Answery = log 42 ≈ 1.6232

A sanity check without the calculator: \(10^1 = 10\) and \(10^2 = 100\), and 42 sits between them, so \(y\) must be between 1 and 2.

Solve \(15e^{1 - x} = 8\).

Show solution

Isolate the exponential first, then switch to log form.

  1. Divide by 15\[e^{1-x} = \frac{8}{15}\]
  2. Switchthe base is \(e\), so the logarithm is \(\ln\): \[\ln\!\left(\frac{8}{15}\right) = 1 - x\]
  3. Solve\[x = 1 - \ln\!\left(\frac{8}{15}\right) \approx 1.6286\]

Answerx = 1 − ln(8/15) ≈ 1.629

Check: \(15e^{1 - 1.6286} = 15e^{-0.6286} = 8\). ✓

Divide before you take the logarithm

The 15 is multiplying the exponential, so it comes off by division, not by taking a logarithm of part of the expression. Only once the equation reads \(e^{\text{something}} = \text{number}\) can you switch forms.

Note also that \(\ln\frac{8}{15}\) is negative, since \(\frac{8}{15} < 1\). That is why \(1 - \ln\frac{8}{15}\) comes out bigger than 1.

NotationNotation warnings

A logarithm never appears in a formula without an input. There is no such thing as a bare “\(\ln\)” standing alone, the way a number can stand alone.

Two things you cannot do

\(\ln x = 4\) does not mean \(x = \frac{4}{\ln}\).

\(\sqrt{x} = 4\) does not mean \(x = \frac{4}{\sqrt{\phantom{x}}}\).

In both cases \(\ln x\) and \(\sqrt{x}\) mean applying a function to \(x\), not multiplying \(x\) by something. There is nothing to divide by.

This is the same point as \(f(x)\) not meaning \(f\) times \(x\), from section 2.1. To undo a function you apply its inverse: \(\ln x = 4\) gives \(x = e^4\), and \(\sqrt{x} = 4\) gives \(x = 16\).
