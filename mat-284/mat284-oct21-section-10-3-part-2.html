The quotient rule

Warm-upMarginal revenue from a price-demand equation

Given the price-demand equation \(p = 300 \cdot 0.9^x\), find the marginal revenue function.

This is a two-step problem:

  1. Find the total revenue: price · quantity.
  2. Marginal revenue = derivative of total revenue.
Show solution
  1. Revenue\(R(x) = p \cdot x = 300 \cdot 0.9^x \cdot x = 300x \cdot (0.9)^x\)
  2. Structurea product of \(300x\) and \((0.9)^x\), so use the product rule: \[R'(x) = (300x)' \cdot (0.9)^x + (300x)\left((0.9)^x\right)'\]
  3. Computewith \((b^x)' = b^x \ln b\): \[R'(x) = 300 \cdot (0.9)^x + (300x)(0.9)^x \ln(0.9)\]

AnswerR′(x) = 300(0.9)ˣ + 300x(0.9)ˣ ln(0.9)

Variable exponent

The derivative of \((0.9)^x\) is \((0.9)^x \ln(0.9)\), not \(x \cdot 0.9^{x-1}\). That is the power rule, which is for a constant exponent. Here the exponent is the variable.

RuleThe quotient rule

The rule for the derivative of a quotient is a bit more complicated than for products. As in the book, it is stated here using \(T\) and \(B\) for the top and bottom terms.

Worth memorizing

  • \[\left(\frac{T}{B}\right)' = \frac{T'B - TB'}{B^2}\]

Compare with the product rule, \((TB)' = T'B + TB'\). Some people like to write the quotient rule as \(\left(\dfrac{T}{B}\right)' = \dfrac{BT' - TB'}{B^2}\), which is the same thing. Remember that the term with (bottom)′ is subtracted.

To remember this minus sign, it may help to consider that the growth of the denominator \(B\) contributes negatively to the growth of the quotient.

How does the GDP per capita change if (a) GDP grows with constant population; (b) population grows with constant GDP?

Show solution

GDP per capita is a quotient, and the quotient rule says

\[\left(\frac{\text{GDP}}{\text{population}}\right)' = \frac{(\text{GDP})' \cdot \text{population} - \text{GDP} \cdot (\text{population})'}{(\text{population})^2}\]
  1. (a)only the first term of the numerator is nonzero, and it is positive: GDP per capita goes up
  2. (b)only the subtracted term is nonzero: GDP per capita goes down

Answer(a) increases  ·  (b) decreases

Try the quotient rule on the function \(y = \dfrac{15}{x}\).

Show solution
  1. Quotient rule\[\left(\frac{15}{x}\right)' = \frac{(15)' \cdot x - 15 \cdot (x)'}{x^2} = \frac{0 \cdot x - 15 \cdot 1}{x^2} = -\frac{15}{x^2}\]
  2. Shortcutbecause \(\dfrac{15}{x} = 15x^{-1}\): \((15x^{-1})' = 15(-1)x^{-2} = -\dfrac{15}{x^2}\)

Answer−15/x²

In this example the quotient rule was unnecessary. Note that \(0 \cdot \text{anything} = 0\), so a constant on top always loses the first term.

A more reasonable example: find the derivative of \(y = \dfrac{5x}{x^2 + 4}\).

Show solution
  1. Top, bottom\(T = 5x\) and \(B = x^2 + 4\)
  2. Quotient rule\[y' = \frac{(5x)'(x^2+4) - 5x(x^2+4)'}{(x^2+4)^2} = \frac{5(x^2+4) - 5x(2x)}{(x^2+4)^2}\]
  3. If neededsimplify the numerator: \[\frac{5x^2 + 20 - 10x^2}{(x^2+4)^2} = \frac{20 - 5x^2}{(x^2+4)^2}\]

Answery′ = (20 − 5x²)/(x² + 4)²

Leave the denominator alone

\((x^2 + 4)^2 \ne x^4 + 16\). Multiplying out the denominator would be useless anyway: it does not make the answer any simpler.

JudgmentWhen (not) to use the quotient rule

Division by a constant does not need the quotient rule.

Find \(\left(\dfrac{x^7}{5}\right)'\).

Show solution
  1. Rewrite\(\dfrac{x^7}{5}\) is really \(\dfrac15 x^7\), so \(\left(\dfrac15 x^7\right)' = \dfrac15 (x^7)' = \dfrac15 \cdot 7x^6 = \dfrac75 x^6\)
  2. Shorter\(\left(\dfrac{x^7}{5}\right)' = \dfrac{(x^7)'}{5} = \dfrac{7x^6}{5}\)

Answer(7/5)x⁶

There is also no need for the quotient rule when the quotient can easily be rewritten as a product. Remember how negative exponents work: division by \(x^n\) is multiplication by \(x^{-n}\).

Find the derivatives of (a) \(\dfrac{e^x}{x^2}\) and (b) \(\dfrac{3}{x^4}\).

Show solution
  1. (a)\(\dfrac{e^x}{x^2} = x^{-2}e^x\), so by the product rule \[(x^{-2}e^x)' = (x^{-2})'e^x + x^{-2}(e^x)' = -2x^{-3}e^x + x^{-2}e^x\]
  2. (b)\(\dfrac{3}{x^4} = 3x^{-4}\), so the derivative is \((3x^{-4})' = 3(-4)x^{-5} = -12x^{-5}\)

Answer(a) −2x⁻³eˣ + x⁻²eˣ   (b) −12x⁻⁵

Can we avoid the quotient rule in the following problems? (a) \(\left(\dfrac{x^5 + 3}{x^2}\right)'\)    (b) \(\left(\dfrac{x^2}{x^5 + 3}\right)'\)

Show solution
  1. (a) yessplit the fraction, since \(\dfrac{A + B}{C} = \dfrac{A}{C} + \dfrac{B}{C}\): \[\left(\frac{x^5}{x^2} + \frac{3}{x^2}\right)' = (x^3 + 3x^{-2})' = 3x^2 + 3(-2)x^{-3} = 3x^2 - 6x^{-3}\]
  2. (b) nounfortunately, \(\dfrac{A}{B + C} \ne \dfrac{A}{B} + \dfrac{A}{C}\). So use the quotient rule: \[\frac{(x^2)'(x^5+3) - (x^2)(x^5+3)'}{(x^5+3)^2} = \frac{(2x)(x^5+3) - (x^2)(5x^4)}{(x^5+3)^2}\]
  3. (b) or\[\frac{2x^6 + 6x - 5x^6}{(x^5+3)^2} = \frac{6x - 3x^6}{(x^5+3)^2}\]

Answer(a) 3x² − 6x⁻³   (b) (6x − 3x⁶)/(x⁵ + 3)²

Which fractions split

A sum on top splits over a single term on the bottom. A sum on the bottom does not split. That one difference decides whether you need the quotient rule.

If a quotient contains another quotient, it’s better to do some algebra first to simplify the function. You don’t want to use the quotient rule multiple times.

Find \(f'(x)\) for \(f(x) = \dfrac{x + 3x^2}{3 - 4/x}\).

Show solution
  1. Rewrite\(f(x) = \dfrac{x + 3x^2}{3 - 4x^{-1}}\): now only one quotient rule is needed
  2. Quotient rule\[f'(x) = \frac{(x + 3x^2)'(3 - 4x^{-1}) - (x + 3x^2)(3 - 4x^{-1})'}{(3 - 4x^{-1})^2}\]
  3. Compute\[= \frac{(1 + 6x)(3 - 4x^{-1}) - (x + 3x^2)(-4)(-1)x^{-2}}{(3 - 4x^{-1})^2}\]

Answerf′(x) = [(1 + 6x)(3 − 4x⁻¹) − 4x⁻²(x + 3x²)] / (3 − 4x⁻¹)²

Another way to do the algebra

You can also clear the inner fraction by multiplying the top and the bottom by \(x\): \(f(x) = \dfrac{x^2 + 3x^3}{3x - 4}\). The quotient rule then gives \(\dfrac{(2x + 9x^2)(3x - 4) - 3(x^2 + 3x^3)}{(3x - 4)^2} = \dfrac{18x^3 - 33x^2 - 8x}{(3x - 4)^2}\), the same derivative in a tidier form.

Big pictureWhich rule to use?

The key to finding derivatives is the correct identification of a given function.

Rules so far
The function isIts derivative is
Sum or difference: \((\square) \pm (\square)\)\((\square)' \pm (\square)'\)
Something with a constant factor, like \(7 \cdot (\square)\)\(7 \cdot (\square)'\)
Just a constant, like \(7\)\(0\)
Power of the variable: \(x^a\)\(a x^{a-1}\)
Product: \((\square) \cdot (\blacksquare)\)\((\square)' \cdot (\blacksquare) + (\square) \cdot (\blacksquare)'\)
Quotient: \((\square) / (\blacksquare)\)\(\big((\square)' \cdot (\blacksquare) - (\square) \cdot (\blacksquare)'\big) / (\blacksquare)^2\)

In short

  • Sum or difference: distribute the derivative.
  • Constant factor: pull it out of the derivative.
  • Power of \(x\), or something that can be written as a power of \(x\): \((x^a)' = ax^{a-1}\).
  • Product: use \(F'S + FS'\).
  • Quotient: use \((T'B - TB')/B^2\), with the minus in front of the term with \(B'\).

Other structures will appear in 10.4 (the chain rule), but that will be after Exam 3.

One has to recognize the overall structure of a formula without getting lost in the details. Some WeBWorK problems help by hiding the details.

Suppose that \(H(x) = f(x)/g(x)\), where \(g(4) = -9\), \(g'(4) = -2\), \(f(4) = 3\), \(f'(4) = -1\). Find \(H'(4)\).

Show solution
  1. Rule\(H'(x) = \dfrac{f'(x)g(x) - f(x)g'(x)}{(g(x))^2}\)
  2. Plug in 4\[H'(4) = \frac{f'(4)g(4) - f(4)g'(4)}{(g(4))^2} = \frac{(-1)(-9) - (3)(-2)}{(-9)^2} = \frac{9 + 6}{81} = \frac{15}{81} = \frac{5}{27}\]

AnswerH′(4) = 5/27

PracticeMore practice on derivative rules

Find the derivative of (a) \(f(x) = \dfrac{x}{2x + 1}\) and (b) \(h(z) = \dfrac{\ln z}{z^2 + 4}\).

Show solution
  1. (a)\[f'(x) = \frac{(x)'(2x+1) - (x)(2x+1)'}{(2x+1)^2} = \frac{1(2x+1) - x(2)}{(2x+1)^2} = \frac{1}{(2x+1)^2}\]
  2. (b)with \((\ln z)' = \dfrac{1}{z}\): \[h'(z) = \frac{(\ln z)'(z^2+4) - (\ln z)(z^2+4)'}{(z^2+4)^2} = \frac{\frac{1}{z}(z^2+4) - (\ln z)(2z)}{(z^2+4)^2}\]

Answer(a) 1/(2x + 1)²   (b) [(z² + 4)/z − 2z ln z] / (z² + 4)²

The tempting shortcut

In (a), the answer is not \(\dfrac{(x)'}{(2x+1)'} = \dfrac12\). The derivative does not distribute over quotients, just as it does not distribute over products. And for logarithms with other bases, recall \((\log_b x)' = \dfrac{1}{x \ln b}\).
