WarmupExact or approximate?

For the equation \(17x = 5\), what is the exact solution? What is a decimal approximation?

Show solution
  1. Exactdivide both sides by 17: \(x = \dfrac{5}{17}\)
  2. Decimal\(x \approx 0.2941\)

Answerx = 5/17 exactly; x ≈ 0.2941

Even \(x \approx 0.2941176470588235\), all the digits a calculator shows, is still just an approximation. The decimal expansion of \(5/17\) goes on forever, so any decimal that stops falls short of the exact value.

The same goes for logarithms

The equation \(17^x = 5\) has the exact answer \(x = \dfrac{\ln 5}{\ln 17}\). Anything else, such as \(x \approx 0.5681\), is an approximation.

IdeaFrom secant to tangent

Recall how to find \(\displaystyle \lim_{x\to 3}\frac{x^2-9}{x-3}\).

Show solution
  1. Plug inat \(x = 3\) we get \(\frac{9-9}{3-3} = \frac{0}{0}\), so we must factor and cancel
  2. Factor\[\frac{(x-3)(x+3)}{x-3} = x + 3\]
  3. Limit\(\lim\limits_{x\to 3}(x+3) = 3 + 3 = 6\)

Answer6

This quotient of two differences is the slope of a line through two points on the graph of \(y = x^2\), namely \((3, 9)\) and \((x, x^2)\):

\[\frac{x^2-9}{x-3} = \frac{\text{difference of } y}{\text{difference of } x} = \frac{\text{vertical change}}{\text{horizontal change}} = \text{slope of the line through two points.}\]

As \(x \to 3\), the second point slides along the graph toward the first. The line through them, called a secant line, turns into the tangent line to the graph at \((3, 9)\).

Worth memorizing

  • The slope of a line measures the rate of change of \(y\) with respect to \(x\).
  • So the slope of the tangent line to the graph of a function measures the rate of change of that function.

Since \(\lim\limits_{x\to 3}\frac{x^2-9}{x-3} = 6\), the rate of change of \(x^2\) is equal to 6 when \(x = 3\).

There is nothing special about \(x^2\) here. The same computation works for any function \(y = f(x)\): take two nearby points on the graph, and divide the vertical difference by the horizontal difference. It is usually written like this:

\[\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} \qquad \frac{\text{vertical difference}}{\text{horizontal difference}}\]

Here \(h\) is the difference of the horizontal coordinates, a small number but not zero. The numerator is the difference of the vertical coordinates.

Always 0/0

This is always a \(\frac{0}{0}\) type of limit: as \(h \to 0\), the numerator and the denominator both tend to 0. So plugging in \(h = 0\) never works directly. The algebra has to cancel the \(h\) first, just as the factoring did for \(x^2\).

DefinitionThe derivative

If this limit exists, it is called the derivative of \(f\) at the point \(x\), denoted \(f'(x)\).

Worth memorizing

  • \[f'(x) = \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\]
  • The fraction \(\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\) is called the difference quotient.
  • If the limit does not exist, then the derivative does not exist.

The input variable does not have to be called \(x\).

  • If the position of an object at time \(t\) is \(g(t)\), then its rate of change, the velocity of the object, is \[g'(t) = \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{g(t+h)-g(t)}{h}\]
  • If the price–demand equation is written as \(x = f(p)\), then \[f'(p) = \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(p+h)-f(p)}{h}\] is the rate of change of the demand with respect to price. This quantity will be important when we measure the elasticity of demand later.

Difference of arguments

The denominator \(h\) is the difference of the two inputs: \((t + h) - t = h\). The numerator is the difference of the outputs at those same two inputs.

MethodUsing the definition

The textbook describes finding \(f'(x)\) as a four-step process:

  1. Find \(f(x+h)\).
  2. Find \(f(x+h) - f(x)\).
  3. Find \(\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\).
  4. Find \(\lim\limits_{h\to 0}\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\). This is \(f'(x)\).

In short: substitute, subtract, simplify, let \(h \to 0\).

Most important step

Understand what \(f(x+h)\) means. It does not mean adding \(h\). It means replacing \(x\) with \(x + h\) in the formula for \(f\).

Example 1. Using the definition of derivative, find \(f'(x)\) for \(f(x) = 5x - 3\).

Show solution
  1. Substitute\(f(x+h) = 5(x+h) - 3 = 5x + 5h - 3\)
  2. Subtract\[f(x+h) - f(x) = 5x + 5h - 3 - (5x - 3) = 5x + 5h - 3 - 5x + 3 = 5h\]
  3. Simplify\(\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} = \dfrac{5h}{h} = 5\)
  4. Let h → 0\(\lim\limits_{h\to 0} 5 = 5\)

Answerf′(x) = 5

The rate of change of \(f(x) = 5x - 3\) is always equal to 5: it grows at the constant rate 5. That is also the slope of the line \(y = 5x - 3\).

Example 2. Using the definition of derivative, find \(f'(x)\) for \(f(x) = 2x - 3x^2\).

Show solution
  1. Substitutereplace both \(x\)’s: \(f(x+h) = 2(x+h) - 3(x+h)^2\)
  2. Expandwith \((x+h)(x+h) = x^2 + 2xh + h^2\), \[f(x+h) = 2x + 2h - 3(x^2 + 2xh + h^2) = 2x + 2h - 3x^2 - 6xh - 3h^2\]
  3. Subtract\[f(x+h) - f(x) = 2x + 2h - 3x^2 - 6xh - 3h^2 - (2x - 3x^2) = 2h - 6xh - 3h^2\]
  4. Simplify\[\frac{2h - 6xh - 3h^2}{h} = \frac{h(2 - 6x - 3h)}{h} = 2 - 6x - 3h\]
  5. Let h → 0\(\lim\limits_{h\to 0}(2 - 6x - 3h) = 2 - 6x\)

Answerf′(x) = 2 − 6x

Two places to slip

\((x+h)^2\) is not \(x^2 + h^2\). The middle term \(2xh\) is the one that survives to the end.

Put parentheses around \(f(x)\) when you subtract it, so that every one of its terms changes sign. A built-in check: after subtracting, every term without an \(h\) must cancel. If one is left over, look for the mistake in the first two steps.

ApplicationThe tangent line

Key concept

  • The slope of the tangent line is the value of the derivative at the given \(x\).

For Example 2, the two formulas have different jobs. \(f(x) = 2x - 3x^2\) gives the \(y\)-coordinate of a point on the graph; \(f'(x) = 2 - 6x\) gives the slope of the tangent line there.

For \(f(x) = 2x - 3x^2\) from Example 2, find the tangent line at the point with \(x = 2\).

Show solution
  1. Point\(y = f(2) = 2(2) - 3(2)^2 = 4 - 12 = -8\)
  2. Slope\(f'(2) = 2 - 6(2) = -10\)
  3. Linepoint-slope form \(y - y_1 = m(x - x_1)\) with the point \((2, -8)\) and slope \(-10\)

Answery − (−8) = −10(x − 2), or y = −10x + 12

Y value, slope, x value

In \(y - (-8) = -10(x - 2)\), the \(-8\) is the \(y\) value of the point, the \(-10\) is the slope, and the 2 is the \(x\) value. Plug \(x = 2\) into \(f\) for the point and into \(f'\) for the slope, not the other way around.

GraphsWhen the derivative does not exist

Recall that

\[f'(x) = \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\]

provided this limit exists. But when does it exist?

Three situations when \(f'(x)\) does not exist

  • \(f\) is not continuous at \(x\). This includes not being defined there.
  • The graph of \(f\) has a corner at \(x\).
  • The graph of \(f\) has a vertical tangent line at \(x\). A vertical line has no slope: its slope is undefined.

A. Find all numbers \(x\) such that \(f'(x)\) does not exist, based on the graph below. State the reasons.

Show solution
  1. x = −1a jump: \(f\) is not continuous here. Both pieces end in open circles, so \(f(-1)\) is not even defined.
  2. x = 2a corner: the graph comes down to \((2, 0)\) and leaves going up, with a sharp point in between

Answerx = −1 and x = 2

B. Find all numbers \(x\) such that \(f'(x)\) does not exist, based on the graph below. State the reasons.

Show solution
  1. x = −1a vertical tangent line: the graph turns straight down as it crosses the \(x\)-axis
  2. x = 4a corner: the peak is a sharp point

Answerx = −1 and x = 4

C. Find all numbers \(x\) such that \(f'(x)\) does not exist, based on the graph below. State the reasons.

Show solution
  1. x = 0not continuous: both pieces plunge downward along the \(y\)-axis, a vertical asymptote, and \(f(0)\) is not defined
  2. x = 3a corner: the peak is a sharp point

Answerx = 0 and x = 3

Smooth turning points are fine

The top of the hump in graph A, near \(x = 1\), and the bottom of graph B, just right of the \(y\)-axis, are smooth. The tangent line there is horizontal, so the derivative is 0, and 0 is a perfectly good value. Only breaks, sharp points, and vertical tangents make the derivative fail to exist.
