Warm-up · Section 9.5Combine exponents first

We use the power rule extensively in these WeBWorK problems:

Power rule

  • \(\displaystyle (x^n)' = nx^{n-1}\)
  • For example, \((x^4)' = 4x^3\), and \((x)' = 1\cdot x^0 = 1\).

Find the derivative of \[f(x) = 3x^6\sqrt{x} + \frac{3}{x^2\sqrt{x}}.\]

Algebra: rewrite \(f(x)\), combining exponents. Calculus: find \(f'(x)\) using the rules from 9.5.

Show solution
  1. First termReplace the square root by a power and add exponents: \[3x^6x^{1/2} = 3x^{6+1/2} = 3x^{13/2}.\]
  2. Second termCombine the powers in the denominator, then use a negative exponent: \[\frac{3}{x^2x^{1/2}} = \frac{3}{x^{5/2}} = 3x^{-5/2}.\]
  3. Rewrite\(f(x) = 3x^{13/2} + 3x^{-5/2}\).
  4. DifferentiateApply the sum rule and constant-multiple rule: \[f'(x) = 3(x^{13/2})' + 3(x^{-5/2})'.\] Then use the power rule on each term: \[f'(x) = 3\cdot\frac{13}{2}x^{11/2} + 3\cdot\left(-\frac{5}{2}\right)x^{-7/2}.\]

Answer\(\displaystyle f'(x) = \frac{39}{2}x^{11/2} - \frac{15}{2}x^{-7/2}\)

Subtracting 1 from a negative exponent: \(\displaystyle -\frac{5}{2}-1 = -\frac{5}{2}-\frac{2}{2} = -\frac{7}{2}\).

Warm-up continuedThe derivative gives the slope

Suppose \(f(x) = 7x^2 - 2x\).

  1. Find \(f'(x)\).
  2. Find the slope of the graph at \(x = 3\).
  3. Find an equation of the tangent line at \(x = 3\).
  4. Find all values of \(x\) where the tangent line is horizontal.
Show solution
  1. (a) Derivative\(f'(x) = 7\cdot 2x - 2\cdot 1 = 14x - 2\).
  2. (b) SlopeEvaluate the derivative at \(x=3\): \[f'(3) = 14\cdot 3 - 2 = 40.\]
  3. (c) PointThe original function gives the \(y\)-coordinate: \[f(3) = 7\cdot 3^2 - 2\cdot 3 = 63 - 6 = 57.\] The tangent line passes through \((3,57)\).
  4. Tangent lineUse point-slope form with slope 40: \[y - 57 = 40(x - 3).\]
  5. (d) HorizontalA horizontal tangent has slope zero. Solve \[14x - 2 = 0,\qquad x = \frac{1}{7}.\]

Tangent at x = 3\(y - 57 = 40(x - 3)\)

Horizontal tangent\(\displaystyle x = \frac{1}{7}\)

Keep the roles separate: \(f(3)\) gives the height; \(f'(3)\) gives the slope.

\(\frac{1}{7}\) is exact. The decimal \(0.1429\) is only an approximation.

Section 9.7Marginal cost: estimating one more unit

We now put the derivative in the context of cost and revenue analysis. The total cost function \(C(x)\) measures the cost of producing \(x\) units.

Once \(x\) units are already made, the exact cost of one additional unit is

\[C(x+1)-C(x).\]

Working with this difference can be awkward. For example, if \(C(x)=50\sqrt{x}\), then

\[C(x+1)-C(x)=50\sqrt{x+1}-50\sqrt{x}.\]

This form does not make it immediately clear how the additional cost changes as production increases.

Why use a derivative?

By definition,

\[C'(x)=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{C(x+h)-C(x)}{h}.\]

Putting \(h=1\) into the difference quotient gives \(C(x+1)-C(x)\), the exact cost of the next unit. If the rate of change varies little over that one-unit interval, the derivative is a useful estimate:

\[C(x+1)-C(x)\approx C'(x).\]

Marginal means derivative

The derivative of the total cost function is the marginal cost function \(C'(x)\). It estimates the cost of one additional unit at production level \(x\).

Given \(C(x)=50\sqrt{x}\):

  1. Find the marginal cost function.
  2. Compare marginal cost at \(x=100\) and \(x=400\).
  3. Find the exact cost of the 101st unit and compare it with the marginal estimate.
Show solution
  1. Rewrite\(C(x)=50x^{1/2}\).
  2. Differentiate\(\displaystyle C'(x)=50\cdot\frac12 x^{-1/2}=\frac{25}{\sqrt{x}}\).
  3. At 100 units\(\displaystyle C'(100)=\frac{25}{\sqrt{100}}=2.50\). The next unit costs approximately $2.50.
  4. At 400 units\(\displaystyle C'(400)=\frac{25}{\sqrt{400}}=1.25\). The next unit costs approximately $1.25.
  5. Exact increaseThe 101st unit changes production from 100 to 101: \[\begin{aligned} C(101)-C(100)&=50\sqrt{101}-50\sqrt{100}\\ &=50(\sqrt{101}-10)\\ &\approx 2.493781056. \end{aligned}\]

Marginal cost\(\displaystyle C'(x)=\frac{25}{\sqrt{x}}\)

The exact expression for the cost of the 101st unit is \(50(\sqrt{101}-10)\) dollars, or $2.49 to the nearest cent. The derivative gives the close estimate $2.50.

RevenueDifferentiate total revenue

The total revenue function \(R(x)\) is the revenue from selling \(x\) units. Its derivative \(R'(x)\) is the marginal revenue function.

Find the marginal revenue at \(x=12\), given \[R(x)=x\left(30-\frac{x}{5}\right).\]

Show solution
  1. Expand first\(\displaystyle R(x)=30x-\frac15x^2\). Multiplying out lets us use the rules from 9.5; the product rule from 10.3 is not needed here.
  2. Differentiate\(\displaystyle R'(x)=30-\frac15\cdot 2x=30-\frac25x\).
  3. At x = 12\(\displaystyle R'(12)=30-\frac25\cdot12=30-4.8=25.2\).

Answer\(R'(12)=25.2\), or $25.20 per unit

At a sales level of 12 units, selling one additional unit increases revenue by approximately $25.20.

ProfitRevenue minus cost

The total profit function \(P(x)\) is the profit from producing and selling \(x\) units:

\[P(x)=R(x)-C(x).\]

Its derivative \(P'(x)\) is the marginal profit function.

Use the revenue function from the preceding example. Assume a fixed cost of $500 and a variable cost of $5 per unit. Find the total profit function and the marginal profit function. Compare marginal profit at \(x=40\) and \(x=80\).

Show solution
  1. Total revenue\(\displaystyle R(x)=30x-\frac15x^2\).
  2. Total cost\(C(x)=500+5x\).
  3. SubtractSubtract the entire cost expression: \[\begin{aligned} P(x)&=\left(30x-\frac15x^2\right)-(500+5x)\\ &=30x-\frac15x^2-500-5x\\ &=25x-\frac15x^2-500. \end{aligned}\]
  4. Differentiate\(\displaystyle P'(x)=25-\frac15\cdot2x-0=25-\frac25x\). The fixed cost is constant, so its derivative is zero.
  5. At 40 units\(\displaystyle P'(40)=25-\frac25\cdot40=9>0\).
  6. At 80 units\(\displaystyle P'(80)=25-\frac25\cdot80=-7<0\).

Total profit\(\displaystyle P(x)=25x-\frac15x^2-500\)

Marginal profit\(\displaystyle P'(x)=25-\frac25x\)

At 40 units, one additional unit increases profit by approximately $9. At 80 units, one additional unit decreases profit by approximately $7.

AveragesDivide by the number of units

Average cost per unit = total cost / number of units. We write

\[\overline{C}(x)=\frac{C(x)}{x},\qquad x>0.\]

The line over \(C\) stands for “average.” When the argument \(x\) is understood, we can write \(\overline{C}=C/x\). Similarly, we can compute average revenue \(\overline{R}\) and average profit \(\overline{P}\).

Total, marginal, and average quantities
QuantityTotalMarginalAverage
Cost\(C(x)\)\(C'(x)\)\(C(x)/x\)
Revenue\(R(x)\)\(R'(x)\)\(R(x)/x\)
Profit\(P(x)\)\(P'(x)\)\(P(x)/x\)

Marginal: differentiate the total. Average: divide the total by \(x\). Computing an average requires algebra, not calculus.

If \(C(x)=5000+20x\), find the marginal cost and the average cost.

Show solution
  1. Marginal\(C'(x)=0+20=20\).
  2. Average\(\displaystyle \overline{C}(x)=\frac{5000+20x}{x}=\frac{5000}{x}+20\).

Marginal cost\(C'(x)=20\)

Average cost\(\displaystyle \overline{C}(x)=\frac{5000}{x}+20\)

The fixed cost contributes \(5000/x\) to the average cost of each unit, but contributes zero to marginal cost.

Connecting the quantitiesAverage cost → total cost → marginal cost

To find marginal cost from average cost, first recover total cost. Differentiating average cost directly does not give marginal cost.

Given \[\overline{C}(x)=5+\frac{40}{x}+\frac{100}{x^2},\] find \(C'(x)\).

Show solution
  1. Plan\(\overline{C}\longrightarrow C\longrightarrow C'\).
  2. Recover totalSince \(\overline{C}=C/x\), multiply by \(x\): \[\begin{aligned} C(x)&=x\overline{C}(x)\\ &=5x+40+\frac{100}{x}\\ &=5x+40+100x^{-1}. \end{aligned}\]
  3. Differentiate\(\displaystyle C'(x)=5+0+100(-1)x^{-2}=5-\frac{100}{x^2}\).

Answer\(\displaystyle C'(x)=5-\frac{100}{x^2}\)
