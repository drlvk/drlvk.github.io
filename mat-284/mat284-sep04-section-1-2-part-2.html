MethodA line from two data points

Linear equations are popular in applications because they can be pinned down from just two data points. First compute the slope, then feed it and either one of the points into the point-slope equation.

The two-step recipe

  • \[m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}\]
  • then \[y - y_1 = m(x - x_1)\]

Of course, the assumption that a real-world relation is linear, such as the relation between price and demand, may be true or false in practice. It is an assumption, not a discovery.

Poll question from class

Suppose 10 units cost $40 and 20 units cost $70. Find the cost equation, assuming it is linear.

Show solution

Decide what the variables mean first. Here \(x\) is the number of units, which goes on the horizontal axis, and \(C\) is the cost, which goes on the vertical. So the points are \((10, 40)\) and \((20, 70)\).

  1. Slope\[m = \frac{70 - 40}{20 - 10} = \frac{30}{10} = 3\]
  2. Point-slopethrough \((10, 40)\): \[C - 40 = 3(x - 10)\]
  3. Distribute\[C - 40 = 3x - 30\]
  4. Add 40\[C = 3x + 10\]

AnswerC = 3x + 10

Both numbers have meanings you already know: the slope 3 is the cost per unit, and the intercept 10 is the fixed cost. Check: at \(x = 10\), \(C = 40\), and at \(x = 20\), \(C = 70\). ✓

DefinitionDemand and supply

The price–demand equation shows how much consumers are willing to buy at a given price. It relates the quantity \(x\) demanded to the price \(p\).

The price–supply equation shows how much producers are willing to sell at a given price. It also relates a quantity \(x\) produced to a price \(p\), but it is a different relation.

By convention both are drawn on a coordinate plane with the horizontal axis labeled \(x\) and the vertical axis labeled \(p\). That gives us the demand curve and the supply curve.

The sign of each slope

  • The demand curve has negative slope. Cheaper goods sell in larger numbers.
  • The supply curve has positive slope. Higher prices make producers willing to make more.

A sign check you get for free

Once you have computed a slope, look at its sign before going further. A positive slope on a demand equation, or a negative one on a supply equation, means something went wrong in the setup. It is the cheapest error check in this section.

Worked exampleA linear demand equation

Demand and supply equations are easy to find if we assume they are linear. When a problem makes that assumption, it will say so explicitly. Later in the course we will handle non-linear demand and supply, where calculus becomes necessary.

When a coffee shop charged $4 for a large cappuccino, they sold 350 cups per day. After raising the price to $5.50, they are selling 270 cups per day. Find the price–demand equation, assuming it is linear.

Show solution

Quantity is \(x\) and price is \(p\), so the points are \((350,\ 4)\) and \((270,\ 5.50)\). Note that quantity comes first even though the problem states the price first.

  1. Slope\[m = \frac{5.50 - 4}{270 - 350} = \frac{1.5}{-80} = -0.01875\]
  2. Sign checknegative, as a demand equation should be
  3. Point-slopethrough \((350,\ 4)\): \[p - 4 = -0.01875(x - 350)\]
  4. Distribute\[p - 4 = -0.01875x + 6.5625\]
  5. Add 4\[p = -0.01875x + 10.5625\]

Answerp − 4 = −0.01875(x − 350), or p = −0.01875x + 10.5625

Check both data points: at \(x = 350\), \(p = 4\), and at \(x = 270\), \(p = 5.50\). ✓

Keep the slope exact

The slope here is \(\frac{1.5}{-80} = -\frac{3}{160}\), which is exactly \(-0.01875\). A calculator showing \(-0.0187\) has cut it short, and rounding a slope this small early will visibly move your answer once you multiply it by a quantity in the hundreds. Round at the end, not in the middle.

Worked exampleA linear supply equation

A manufacturer of shoes will make 50,000 pairs when the price is $75, and 35,000 pairs when the price is $60. Find the price–supply equation, assuming it is linear.

Show solution

The points are \((50000,\ 75)\) and \((35000,\ 60)\).

  1. Slope\[m = \frac{75 - 60}{50000 - 35000} = \frac{15}{15000} = \frac{1}{1000}\]
  2. Sign checkpositive, as a supply equation should be
  3. Point-slopethrough \((35000,\ 60)\): \[p - 60 = \frac{1}{1000}(x - 35000)\]
  4. Distribute\[p - 60 = \frac{1}{1000}x - 35\]
  5. Add 60\[p = \frac{1}{1000}x + 25\]

Answerp = x/1000 + 25

Check: at \(x = 50000\), \(p = 50 + 25 = 75\). ✓

A fraction is often tidier than a decimal

\(\frac{1}{1000}\) is exact and easy to multiply by. Writing it as \(0.001\) is equally fine here, but with slopes like \(\frac{1}{3}\) or \(\frac{1}{160}\) the fraction is the only exact form, so keep the habit.

DefinitionThe equilibrium point

The intersection of the price–supply and price–demand curves is the equilibrium point. Its coordinates are the equilibrium quantity and the equilibrium price. It is the one price at which the amount producers want to sell matches the amount consumers want to buy.

How to find it

Solve both equations for the same variable, then set the two right-hand sides equal.

  • If both are \(p = \cdots\), equate them and solve for \(x\), then get \(p\) from either equation.
  • If it is easier to get both into the form \(x = \cdots\), do that instead, equate, and solve for \(p\).

From WeBWorK. Given the price–supply equation \(2x - 200p + 1600 = 0\) and the price–demand equation \(300p + x - 4800 = 0\), find the equilibrium point.

Show solution

Here it is easier to solve each one for \(x\), since \(x\) appears with a simple coefficient in both.

  1. Supply ÷ 2\[x - 100p + 800 = 0\]
  2. Supply for x\[x = 100p - 800\]
  3. Demand for x\[x = -300p + 4800\]
  4. Set equal\[100p - 800 = -300p + 4800\]
  5. Collect\[400p = 5600\]
  6. Solve\[p = \frac{5600}{400} = 14\]
  7. Back for x\[x = 100(14) - 800 = 1400 - 800 = 600\]

Answerx = 600 and p = 14, that is the point (600, 14)

Check in the other equation: \(300(14) + 600 - 4800 = 4200 + 600 - 4800 = 0\). ✓

Solve for whichever variable is easier

Both routes give the same point, so pick the one with less arithmetic. Here \(x\) has coefficient 2 in one equation and 1 in the other, while \(p\) has 200 and 300, so solving for \(x\) is clearly lighter. Also, say which coordinate is which in your answer: \((600, 14)\) means 600 units at $14, not 600 dollars.

MethodThe effect of a tax

The equilibrium shifts when a tax is imposed on the supplier. Suppose the tax is \(t\) dollars per unit. Having sold a unit for \(p\) dollars, the supplier actually receives \(p - t\) dollars. So the supplier's behavior is governed by \(p - t\), not by \(p\).

To include a tax

  • Replace \(p\) by \((p - \text{amount of tax})\) in the price–supply equation.
  • Leave the price–demand equation unchanged.

The demand equation does not change because consumers still pay \(p\); it is only the seller who keeps less.

Same equations as above: price–supply \(2x - 200p + 1600 = 0\) and price–demand \(300p + x - 4800 = 0\), with equilibrium \((600, 14)\). A $3 per unit tax is added on the supplier. Find the new equilibrium.

Show solution
  1. Replace p\(p\) becomes \((p-3)\) in the supply equation: \[2x - 200(p - 3) + 1600 = 0\]
  2. Divide by 2\[x - 100(p - 3) + 800 = 0\]
  3. Expand\[x - 100p + 300 + 800 = 0\]
  4. New supply\[x = 100p - 1100\]
  5. Demandunchanged: \[x = -300p + 4800\]
  6. Set equal\[100p - 1100 = -300p + 4800\]
  7. Collect\[400p = 5900\]
  8. Solve\[p = \frac{5900}{400} = 14.75\]
  9. Back for x\[x = 100(14.75) - 1100 = 1475 - 1100 = 375\]

Answerx = 375 and p = 14.75, that is the point (375, 14.75)

The tax is $3, but the price rose only 75 cents

The price went from $14.00 to $14.75, and the quantity fell from 600 to 375. So the consumer pays $0.75 more per unit, while the supplier now keeps \(14.75 - 3 = 11.75\), which is $2.25 less than before. The two amounts add to the $3 tax, and here the supplier absorbs three quarters of it.

This is worth knowing as a sanity check as much as an economics point. If your new price comes out $3 higher than the old one, you have almost certainly changed the demand equation as well as the supply equation.

Which sign, and where

It is \(p - 3\), not \(p + 3\), because the supplier receives less than the sticker price. And the substitution goes into the supply equation only. Putting it in both, or into the demand equation, is the most common way this problem goes wrong.
